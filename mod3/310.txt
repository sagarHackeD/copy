310. Explain lungs as a purification system and discuss spirometry.
Lungs as a Purification System:
While primarily an organ of gas exchange (adding O_2 and removing CO_2), the respiratory system also acts as a powerful purification and defense system to protect the body from inhaled pathogens, particles, and toxins.
Mechanisms of Pulmonary Purification:
1.  Physical Filtration (Mucociliary Escalator):
    - The airways (trachea to bronchioles) are lined with a mucus layer and ciliated epithelial cells.
    - Mucus traps inhaled particles (dust, pollen, bacteria) and soluble gases.
    - Cilia (hair-like projections) beat in a coordinated, wave-like motion (600-1000 times per minute) to propel the mucus upward toward the pharynx, where it is swallowed (and destroyed in the stomach) or expectorated (coughed out).
2.  Macrophage Defense (Alveolar Purification):
    - Alveolar Macrophages (dust cells) are immune cells residing in the alveoli.
    - They phagocytose (engulf and digest) pathogens, dead cells, and fine particles (e.g., carbon, silica) that manage to reach the deep gas-exchange surfaces.
3.  Humoral Immunity:
    - Secretory Immunoglobulin A (IgA) and antimicrobial peptides (defensins) are secreted in the mucosal lining to neutralize viruses and bacteria.
4.  Reflex Mechanisms:
    - Sneeze (nasal irritation) and Cough (lower airway irritation) forcefully expel irritants and accumulated mucus at high velocity.
5.  CO_2 Excretion (Metabolic Purification):
    - Carbon dioxide is a volatile metabolic acid. By excreting CO_2, the lungs precisely regulate the blood's acid-base balance (pH). This is the primary way the body eliminates metabolic acids (carbonic acid) generated daily.
Spirometry:
Spirometry is the most common pulmonary function test (PFT). It measures the volume and flow of air inhaled and exhaled to assess how well the lungs are functioning.
Textual Diagram of a Spirometry Trace (Flow-Volume Loop):
```
          Flow (L/s)
            ^
            |   (Expiratory Curve)
            |          Peak Expiratory Flow
            |         /\
            |        /  \
            |       /    \   (Descending limb - effort independent)
            |      /      \
            |     /        \
     --------|----/-----\----\---------> Volume (L)
            |   /        (FEV1)  \
            |  /                   \  (Inspiratory Curve - lower)
            | /                     \
            |/                       \
            |
       (Flow-Volume Loop showing obstruction vs restriction)
```
Key Spirometry Measurements:
| Measurement | Definition | Normal Value |
|
| FVC (Forced Vital Capacity) | The maximum volume of air that can be forcefully exhaled after a maximal deep inhalation. | Predicted based on age, sex, height, ethnicity (e.g., ~4.5L for a male). |
| FEV_1 (Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 sec) | The volume of air exhaled in the first second of the FVC maneuver. | ~80% of FVC (e.g., ~3.5L for a male). |
| FEV_1/FVC Ratio | The percentage of the FVC exhaled in the first second. This is the most critical diagnostic ratio. | > 70% (Normal). |
| PEFR (Peak Expiratory Flow Rate) | The maximum flow rate achieved during the forced expiration. | Varies by age/height; measured in L/min. |
Diagnostic Significance (Distinguishing between two main pathologies):
| Condition | FEV_1 | FVC | FEV_1/FVC Ratio | Flow-Volume Loop Pattern | Example Diseases |
|
| Obstructive Lung Disease | Significantly Reduced (airway narrowing slows exhalation). | Normal or Reduced. | < 70% (Low). | Scooped-out expiratory limb; reduced peak flow. | COPD, Asthma, Chronic Bronchitis, Emphysema. |
| Restrictive Lung Disease | Reduced (less volume to blow out). | Reduced (lungs can't expand fully). | Normal or > 80% (Volume is small but airways are open; ratio stays high). | Small, narrow, but steep loop; looks like a smaller version of normal. | Pulmonary Fibrosis, Sarcoidosis, Chest wall deformities, Neuromuscular disease. |
