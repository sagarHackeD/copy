53. Compare human tongue and electronic tongue.
| Feature | Human Tongue | Electronic Tongue (E-tongue) |
|
| Basis of Detection | Biological chemoreceptors (taste receptor cells expressing GPCRs and ion channels) bind specific tastants. | Non-specific, cross-sensitive chemical sensors (electrochemical, optical) generate a global response pattern. |
| Sensitivity | Highly sensitive to certain compounds (e.g., bitter at micromolar levels). | Can be engineered for high sensitivity, often surpassing human thresholds for certain ions. |
| Objectivity | Subjective – influenced by mood, health, prior food intake, and genetic variability (e.g., supertasters). | Objective – produces unbiased, reproducible quantitative data. |
| Fatigue & Adaptation | Suffers from sensory adaptation (reduced response to continuous stimuli) and fatigue. | No fatigue; can operate 24/7 without loss of performance. |
| Dynamic Range | Narrow; cannot tolerate high concentrations of toxins or dangerous chemicals. | Wide; can handle toxic, corrosive, or highly concentrated samples without damage. |
| Specificity | Specific receptors give specific taste qualities (sweet, umami, etc.). | Non-specific; relies on chemometric algorithms to differentiate complex mixtures. |
| Portability | Inherently portable (on the body). | Often bulky lab equipment (though miniaturization is ongoing). |
| Integration | Integrated with olfactory and cognitive systems for flavor perception. | Standalone analytical tool; lacks integration with smell. |
| Cost | Natural (free). | High initial investment and maintenance cost. |
