55. Explain the steps involved in bioprinting.
Bioprinting is an additive manufacturing technique that uses living cells, biochemical factors, and biocompatible materials (bioinks) to create tissue-like structures layer-by-layer.
The Workflow (Three Main Stages):
Stage 1: Pre-processing (Design & Material Selection)
- Medical Imaging: CT or MRI scans are used to capture the 3D anatomy of the target tissue.
- 3D Model Generation: Image data is converted into a digital 3D model using CAD (Computer-Aided Design) software. The model is then sliced into thin layers (G-code).
- Bioink Selection: Choosing the optimal combination of cells (e.g., fibroblasts, keratinocytes, endothelial cells) and scaffold materials (e.g., collagen, alginate, gelatin).
- Cell Culturing: Expanding the required cell types in culture flasks until sufficient numbers are obtained for the print.
Stage 2: Processing (Printing)
- Bioink Loading: The hydrogel/cell mixture (bioink) is loaded into the printhead (syringe or cartridge).
- Layer-by-Layer Deposition: The bioprinter follows the digital blueprint, depositing the bioink precisely onto a collection platform or scaffolding in a controlled environment (specific temperature, humidity, and sterile conditions).
- Crosslinking (Solidification): During or immediately after printing, the structure is stabilized via physical (UV light, temperature) or chemical (ionic bonding, e.g., Ca²⁺ for alginate) crosslinking to maintain its 3D shape.
Stage 3: Post-processing (Maturation)
- Bioreactor Incubation: The printed construct is placed in a bioreactor that mimics physiological conditions (37°C, 5% CO_2, nutrient flow, mechanical stimulation).
- Cellular Maturation: Cells proliferate, migrate, and reorganize within the scaffold, forming extracellular matrix (ECM) and establishing cell-cell connections.
- Fusion: In scaffold-free techniques (e.g., spheroid printing), printed spheroids fuse together to form a solid, cohesive tissue.
- Quality Control: Assessing viability (live/dead staining), mechanical properties (tensile strength), and functionality (e.g., contraction for cardiac tissue).
